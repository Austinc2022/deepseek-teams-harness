# The Masterwork Standard

"It runs" is where the work starts. A masterwork is something a person remembers, shows to someone else, and
cannot easily find elsewhere. You get there by deciding what the work is for, refusing what does not serve
that, and repeating the loop of making and judging more times than is comfortable.

## 1. Pillars before production

Before anything is built, write the pillars: three to five things this work must be great at. A pillar is a
promise to the audience, stated so that a result can be checked against it.

| Weak pillar | Strong pillar |
|---|---|
| "Fun gameplay" | "Every throw feels heavy: the player leans their body into it within the first minute" |
| "Great visuals" | "One palette of four colours; any frame is recognisable as this work from a thumbnail" |
| "Good story" | "The audience knows what the hero wants and what it will cost by minute five" |

Then write the **anti-pillars**: what this work will not try to be. Scope that is not cut on purpose gets cut by
accident, and the accident always takes the polish.

## 2. Taste is a method

Taste is not a gift. It is specific attention, practised.

- **Study the best.** Name the three finest works in the field. For each, say precisely what it does that
  others do not. Learn the principle; never copy the surface.
- **Find the one thing.** Great work is usually great at one thing and disciplined everywhere else. Decide what
  that one thing is and spend disproportionately on it.
- **Cohesion over quantity.** Everything should look and sound as if one mind made it. Remove whatever belongs
  to a different work, however good it is.
- **First contact.** Judge the first ten seconds and the first minute separately and harshly. Most of the
  audience decides there.
- **Feel.** Response to every action: motion, sound, weight, timing. Flat response reads as cheap, whatever
  else is true.
- **Restraint.** When in doubt, remove. Ask of every element: would anyone miss it?

## 3. The critique loop

Nothing ships on a first pass. The loop:

1. **Build** to the pillars.
2. **Critique** with several independent critics, each holding one lens: craft, audience, engineering, market,
   and the gate's own rubric. Critics read the work itself, never the maker's description of it.
3. **Sort** what comes back:
   - *Must fix*: breaks a pillar, breaks the experience, or is false.
   - *Would elevate*: lifts good to great.
   - *Noise*: a preference that serves no pillar. Drop it.
4. **Fix** every must-fix item, and the two or three elevating changes with the most effect.
5. **Critique again.** Stop when the panel finds nothing that must be fixed and the weakest lens is above the
   bar.

Two rules keep the loop honest. The maker never grades their own work. And a score is only as good as its
reasons: a number without a named defect or a named strength is discarded.

## 4. The polish order

When time is short, cut in this order and no other:

1. Cut features that serve no pillar.
2. Cut content: fewer levels, scenes or options, each finished.
3. Shorten. A complete ten minutes beats an unfinished hour.
4. Never cut feel, the first minute, or correctness.

## 5. Finished

Work is finished when all of these hold:

- Every pillar is met, and you can point to where.
- A stranger can use it from a cold start without help.
- No placeholder, no dead control, no known crash, no claim it cannot support.
- The weakest part is good. Audiences remember the worst moment.
- You would put the company's name on it.

## 6. Honesty about the level

Say where the work actually stands: a prototype, a vertical slice, a release candidate, a finished product, or
a masterwork. Calling a slice a masterpiece costs the company its judgement. State the gap and what would close
it.
