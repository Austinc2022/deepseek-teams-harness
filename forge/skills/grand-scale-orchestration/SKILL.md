# Grand-Scale Orchestration

Large work fails in predictable ways: nobody holds the whole, parts are built that do not fit, integration is
left to the end, and the plan is not updated when reality changes. The method below is how big productions
(films, large games, large systems) avoid those failures. It scales down as well as up.

## 1. Hold the whole in one page

Before any ticket, write the one-page vision and keep it current:

- What it is, for whom, and why it will matter.
- The pillars (see masterwork-standard).
- The shape: the major parts and how they meet.
- What is out of scope.

Every brief you write later quotes from this page. If a brief cannot be traced to it, the work is drifting.

## 2. Phases, each with an exit

| Phase | Purpose | Exit: you may leave when |
|---|---|---|
| Concept | Decide what it is | The vision page exists and survived an attack on it |
| Prototype | Prove the riskiest idea | The core works in the roughest possible form, and is good |
| Vertical slice | Prove the quality bar | One small piece is at final quality, end to end |
| Production | Make the rest | Every part exists and is integrated |
| Polish | Make it excellent | The critique panel finds nothing that must be fixed |
| Release candidate | Prove it ships | It passes the gate |

Never enter production before the slice. The slice tells you what "done" costs; without it every estimate is a
guess. Find the riskiest assumption and test it first, cheaply.

## 3. Workstreams with interfaces

Split the work so that streams can run at the same time without waiting for each other.

- A **workstream** is one coherent area with one owner: for a game, world, mechanics, characters, interface,
  audio, tools; for a film, story, design, animation, lighting, sound, edit.
- Define the **interface** between streams first: file layout, formats, names, the functions or data one stream
  hands to another. Put it in a shared contract that every brief includes. Streams built without a contract do
  not fit.
- Order by **dependency**. Draw what must exist before what. Anything on the longest chain is critical: start
  it first, and give it your strongest builder.
- Keep each ticket small enough to finish and verify in a few turns. A ticket nobody can finish is a plan
  nobody made.

## 4. Integrate early, integrate always

- Create an **integration** ticket at the start, not the end. It depends on the streams and produces the one
  build that contains everything.
- Integrate at every milestone, even when parts are stubs. A stub that fits is worth more than a finished part
  that does not.
- The integrated build is the only truth. Critique and the gate judge that build, never a part on its own.

## 5. The brief

A brief is good when someone who has seen nothing else could deliver it.

- **Goal**: what this ticket achieves for the whole.
- **Contract**: the interfaces it must honour, and the earlier work it builds on.
- **Standard**: the pillars it serves and the quality reference.
- **Done when**: checks a third party can run.
- **Not in scope**: what to leave alone.

## 6. Design the organisation

Structure follows the work.

- One leader per area. When an area is large, give it its own lead (a super agent below you) with its own
  teams, and delegate the area, not the tasks.
- Match talent to difficulty: the strongest model on the critical chain and the hardest design problems;
  faster, cheaper models on routine work.
- Keep reviewers independent of makers. The critic of a thing must not be the builder of it.
- Add a role only when a gap blocks the work. Remove what is not earning its place. A small organisation that
  communicates beats a large one that does not.

## 7. Run it

- **Parallel by default.** Anything not blocked by a dependency starts now.
- **Cadence.** At each milestone: integrate, critique, re-plan. Re-planning is the job, not a failure.
- **Critical chain first.** When capacity is short, it goes to whatever everything else is waiting for.
- **Kill early.** A stream that is not converging is cut or rescoped at the milestone, not at the end.
- **Risk list.** Keep the five things most likely to sink the work, with what you are doing about each.

## 8. Honest scale

Say what scale is actually within reach. The largest productions take thousands of people and years, and use
things agents cannot make: performance capture, recorded orchestras, licensed technology, live audiences. With a
day and a few builders, the honest target is a complete, small work at a high standard, or one vertical slice of
a large one. Plan the large work as a programme of milestones, each of which is real and finished, and tell the
owner where on that road the work stands.
